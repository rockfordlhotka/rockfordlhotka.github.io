When people talk about an "AI bubble" they usually mean the financial kind. That's not the bubble I've been thinking about lately. I've been thinking about the one I live in.

I keep noticing a disconnect between those of us who are AI enthusiasts (and I'm definitely one of them) and the majority of people. Even the majority of developers.

When I'm talking to other speakers, developers, or colleagues who are fully "in the AI space", we have a whole set of base assumptions about how we work. We don't spend time debating whether AI is useful. Instead we compare notes and tips and tricks to help each other improve in how we build software, or use AI for all sorts of other things like meeting prep, document authoring, legal questions, and so on.

Then I go to "normal" events or settings. Software conferences, get-togethers with friends, all sorts of places where I interact with folks. Conversations still sometimes revolve around AI, but in an _entirely_ different way.

Lots of people haven't actually used AI, and have formed opinions based on what's in the media (for good or ill).

Other people have tried AI and found it wanting. Often that was months or years ago, and they usually assume AI hasn't changed much since then. In fact, it changes weekly, sometimes daily.

None of this, by itself, is inherently a good or bad thing. People are busy, and not everyone needs to be on the bleeding edge of every technology.

But it is _so very easy_ for those of us who use AI every day, and who mostly interact with others who embrace AI, to forget that most of the world _isn't_ conversant in AI and what it can (and can't) do at this point in time.

## The media

And so much of what's in the media is b.s. A lot of it exists to sell subscriptions or get clicks. Sensationalism built on half-truths (or less).

Sure, some of it is accurate. And even the half-truths have some truth, of course. But if your whole picture of AI comes from headlines, you are either going to think it is about to replace everyone, or that it is a useless hallucination machine. Neither matches what I see using it every day.

The thing is, it is virtually impossible to understand the strengths and weaknesses of AI without actually using it.

## "Using it" isn't one thing

And "using it" varies by use case and tool.

For example, I mostly use Anthropic tools. Claude has memory, so over time it has become quite good at anticipating what I want and how I want it to work. When I switch to another tool, it is like I'm starting over from months ago, because I have to teach the new tooling how I work.

[STORY: a recent time switching tools felt like starting over]

The same is true with building up AI skills. And I mean that two ways:

1. _My skills_ in using the tools - knowing how to frame a task, when to trust the result, when to push back
1. The skills the agent develops about how to do _its_ work in my world, with my projects and my conventions

Both of those take time to build, and neither transfers cleanly from one tool to another.

So when someone tells me they tried AI and it wasn't very good, my first questions are: which tool, when, doing what, and for how long? Someone who spent an afternoon with a generic chatbot a year ago, and someone who has spent months working with an agent that knows their codebase, have had fundamentally different experiences. Yet both would honestly say they've "used AI".
